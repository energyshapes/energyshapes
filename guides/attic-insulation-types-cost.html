Attic insulation: materials, performance and cost factors

The right attic insulation depends on the existing roof and ceiling assembly, climate, access, moisture conditions and target thermal performance. Learn how common materials differ and what a complete installation quote should include.

Attic insulation is not simply a question of choosing the thickest product. A durable upgrade considers heat flow, air leakage, moisture, ventilation, access and the condition of the existing roof and ceiling. The work should be planned as part of the whole building, not as an isolated pile of material.

What R-value tells you—and what it does not

R-value describes a material's resistance to heat flow: a higher R-value means greater resistance under the stated test conditions. For an installed attic system, the overall result also depends on thickness, continuity, compression, gaps, thermal bridges, wind washing and air movement through or around the insulation. A product label is not a guarantee that the installed attic will perform to that number.

Recommended insulation levels vary by climate zone, building code, roof design and whether the attic floor or roofline forms the thermal boundary. Do not copy an R-value from a different country or climate. Check your local building authority or energy agency, and ask the installer to state the existing and proposed total R-value and the method used to estimate it. The U.S. Department of Energy explains R-value and material considerations in its Consumer Guide to Home Insulation.

Common attic insulation types

Fiberglass batts and rolls

Fiberglass batts are supplied in flexible pre-cut or roll form and are often installed between framing members. They can be practical where the attic floor is accessible and framing bays are regular. The material needs to fit around joists and obstructions without large gaps or compression. Poorly fitted batts, voids around wiring or framing and wind washing can reduce the performance of the assembly. Batts are less convenient around crowded or irregular spaces.

Blown-in fiberglass

Loose-fill fiberglass is installed with blowing equipment and can cover an attic floor more continuously around joists and irregular shapes. It can be useful for topping up existing insulation or filling difficult-to-reach areas, provided the attic is prepared correctly. Installers should use depth markers and follow product coverage instructions so the intended amount is distributed consistently.

Blown-in cellulose

Cellulose is generally manufactured from processed recycled paper fibre and installed as loose fill in attics or by specialist methods in enclosed cavities. Its density and ability to settle into irregular areas can make it useful for attic floors with many obstructions. As with any fibrous insulation, the assembly must be protected from water leaks and installed to the specified density and depth. Cellulose does not make a roof leak harmless or replace a proper air barrier.

Rigid boards and spray-applied products

Rigid foam boards and spray-applied foam may be considered for specific roofline, access or detailing situations. They are not interchangeable with loose-fill insulation and may alter drying potential, ventilation strategy or fire-protection requirements. Their use should be designed for the actual roof assembly and installed by appropriately trained professionals. Foam products can require careful attention to vapour control, thermal barriers and manufacturer instructions.

Air sealing and ventilation belong in the plan

Insulation slows heat flow through the building envelope; it does not automatically seal every crack. Air can move through gaps around attic hatches, plumbing and wiring penetrations, top plates, exhaust ducts, chimneys and other openings. That movement can carry heat and moisture into the attic and bypass insulation. A thorough project identifies appropriate leakage paths and seals them with materials suitable for the location and fire-safety requirements.

Air sealing and ventilation must be considered together. A roof assembly needs a deliberate moisture-management strategy, including correct bathroom and kitchen exhaust termination, suitable attic ventilation where required and safe combustion-air arrangements for fuel-burning appliances. Blocking soffit vents with insulation or sealing around hot flues with ordinary foam can create serious problems. Ask the contractor how ventilation paths and required clearances will be preserved.

The U.S. Department of Energy's Consumer Guide to Air Sealing explains why air sealing must be paired with proper ventilation. The guide is U.S.-specific in some details, so local requirements still apply.

What changes the installed cost?

Quotes can differ because of the existing insulation condition, access, roof geometry, joist depth, required total R-value, material selection, labour rates and local code requirements. Other cost factors may include removing wet or damaged insulation, treating a verified pest issue, building safe access, installing attic-hatch covers, air sealing, ventilation baffles, protecting recessed lights and flues, moving stored items and disposing of waste.

Some installers price by area and material depth, while others quote a complete scope. Ask for the attic area, product name, coverage rate, installed depth, estimated total R-value, preparation tasks and exclusions. A price per square foot is not meaningful without knowing what is included. Do not compare a quote that only adds loose fill with one that includes air sealing, safe clearances and removal of damaged material as if they were equivalent.

What to check before the installer starts

  • Are there active roof leaks, condensation, mould-like growth or damp timbers that need diagnosis first?
  • Are bathroom and kitchen exhausts discharged outdoors rather than into the attic?
  • Are recessed lights, chimneys, flues, electrical equipment and junction boxes subject to required clearances or protective enclosures?
  • Will soffit or other ventilation paths remain clear where the design requires them?
  • Will attic access, storage platforms and service equipment remain safe and accessible?
  • Will the contractor provide photographs, depth markers and a written description of the installed product and coverage?

Request these details in your quote

  • Existing condition and preparation work.
  • Exact insulation material and product specification.
  • Target R-value or local equivalent, plus installed depth.
  • Air sealing, baffles and moisture-management scope.
  • Safe clearances, waste removal, warranty and final documentation.
